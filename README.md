# Fairness in AI-Powered Recruitment: A Study on Screening Outcomes in the UK Job Market

 A mixed-methods investigation into bias and fairness 
in AI-powered recruitment systems, focusing on international graduates 
in the UK labour market.

## Overview
This dissertation examines whether automated CV screening tools treat 
candidates fairly, with particular focus on visa sponsorship status, 
name origin, and keyword optimisation as structural barriers in UK hiring.
It contributes to international debates on algorithmic fairness by 
identifying visa-related filtering as a neglected dimension absent from 
US, EU, and Canadian regulatory frameworks.

## Methodology

**CV Audit Experiment**
- 36 synthetic CVs created, systematically varying visa type (Graduate, 
  Skilled Worker, Student), name origin, and keyword optimisation
- Assessed through a simulated ATS across Technology, Finance, and 
  Healthcare sectors

**Perception Survey**
- 120 job seekers (international and domestic) surveyed on experiences 
  of fairness, transparency, sponsorship concerns, and ATS awareness

## Key Findings

**Optimisation vs Visa Status**
- Keyword optimisation was the strongest determinant of ATS rankings, 
  producing the largest score differences (+0.18)
- Visa status had a modest but present effect: Graduate > Skilled > Student
- Name origin had minimal measurable effect on screening outcomes

**Fairness and Transparency**
- 71.7% of respondents judged automated systems unfair
- 96.7% were aware of ATS use, yet awareness did not reduce scepticism
- Rapid rejections without feedback amplified perceptions of exclusion, 
  particularly among international candidates

**Sectoral Variation**
- Technology: optimised Skilled Worker Visa profiles ranked highest
- Finance: Graduate Visa profiles topped the list
- Healthcare: a Student Visa holder with a non-Anglicised name ranked 
  first, reflecting the sector's reliance on international staff
- Healthcare respondents viewed visa as one factor among many; 
  Technology respondents identified it as the primary barrier

**The Fairness Gap**
- A persistent gap exists between perceptions and measurable outcomes
- Candidates underestimated the importance of optimisation while 
  overestimating the impact of visa and name bias
- Perceptions of opacity act as barriers even where algorithms appear 
  relatively neutral, discouraging applications from international graduates

## Conclusion
Fairness in AI recruitment is both technical and experiential. Visa-related 
filtering introduces additional barriers for international graduates that 
heighten mistrust. Recommendations:
- Mandatory transparency requirements for ATS systems
- Regular algorithmic auditing covering visa-related filtering
- Clearer employer guidance on visa eligibility in job postings
- Structured feedback mechanisms, even in automated form

## Regulatory Context
Current frameworks (NYC Local Law 144, EU AI Act, Canadian OHRC 
guidance) mandate bias audits focused on gender and race but none 
explicitly address visa sponsorship filtering. This study argues visa 
status is a neglected variable in global algorithmic fairness debates.
